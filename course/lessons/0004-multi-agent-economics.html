When multi-agent pays, and when it burns money

A lead agent with subagents is the most expensive architecture in the catalogue and sometimes the only one that works. The exam wants you to know which situation you are in before you spend.

~18 minutes Domain 1, 17% of the exam 5 drills

Lesson 0001 gave you the escalation ladder up to a single agent. This lesson covers the last rung, and it is the one with a price tag attached. Multi-agent is not a harder version of agent. It is a different bet: you are paying roughly four times more tokens again for the chance to explore several directions at once.

The win by the end of this page: you can say the cost multiplier out loud, name the two or three properties of the task that justify it, and spot the scenarios where fanning out actively makes the answer worse.

KnowledgeThe multiplier, and what buys it back

The numbers come from Anthropic's How we built our multi-agent research system, and they are the numbers the exam is written against.

Token cost relative to a chat interaction
ShapeRoughly costsWhat you are buying
Chat interaction 1x, the baseline One turn, one context, no tools
Single agent About 4x A tool loop and multi-step work in one context
Multi-agent About 15x Several context windows explored in parallel

What the 15x buys is measurable. A lead running Claude Opus 4 with Claude Sonnet 4 subagents outperformed single-agent Opus 4 by 90.2% on Anthropic's internal research eval. Token usage on its own explained 80% of the performance variance on BrowseComp, and parallel tool calling cut research time by up to 90% on complex queries. So the spend is not waste. It is the mechanism.

The whole lesson in one sentence

Multi-agent buys you parallel context windows, so it only pays when the work genuinely splits into independent lines of enquiry and the answer is worth fifteen times a chat.

Why parallel context is the real product

A subagent runs in its own window with its own tools and its own trajectory. It can read far more than the lead ever could, then hand back a condensed result. Anthropic's wording: subagents "facilitate compression by operating in parallel with their own context windows, exploring different aspects of the question simultaneously before condensing the most important tokens for the lead research agent". Claude Code's subagent documentation makes the same trade concrete: the subagent does the noisy work in its own context and "returns only the summary". Tens of thousands of tokens of searching can come back as a few paragraphs.

The second effect is separation of concerns: "distinct tools, prompts, and exploration trajectories", which "reduces path dependency". Two subagents are less likely to make the same wrong turn than one agent making two passes.

The fit test

Where it wins and where it loses
Multi-agent wins whenMulti-agent loses when
The query is breadth-first, with several independent directions to pursue at once Every agent needs to share the same context
The work parallelises heavily and wall clock matters There are many dependencies between the agents' work
The information to read exceeds a single context window The agents would have to coordinate in real time, which they are not yet good at
There are numerous complex tools to interface with Most coding work, which has fewer truly parallelisable tasks than research
The value of the task is high enough to pay for the performance Cost or latency is the binding constraint rather than coverage

That last left-hand row is the one to memorise verbatim, because it is the sentence the exam paraphrases: "multi-agent systems require tasks where the value of the task is high enough to pay for the increased performance". Value per task, not difficulty per task.

Sizing, once you have decided

Anthropic embeds explicit scaling rules in the lead agent's prompt, and they are examinable as a rubric:

  • Simple fact-finding: 1 agent, 3 to 10 tool calls.
  • Direct comparison: 2 to 4 subagents, 10 to 15 calls each.
  • Complex research: more than 10 subagents with clearly divided responsibilities.

In steady state a lead typically spins up 3 to 5 subagents in parallel, each using 3 or more tools in parallel of its own. If a scenario describes a lead spawning one subagent, or twenty for a lookup, the sizing is the defect.

Why this bites experienced people. If you run Claude Code all day, spawning subagents feels free, because you are watching wall clock rather than a bill and your own tasks are usually worth the tokens. Two things break that intuition on the exam. First, most of what you delegate is coding work, which is the weakest case for fan-out. Second, the parallelism you like is often really about keeping your main context clean, and that is a context engineering win you can get from a single subagent, not a reason to build a multi-agent architecture for a client.

Doing the arithmetic out loud

The examinable skill is the sentence, not the number. Practise saying it in this shape:

  1. Baseline. "A single agent on this is roughly 4x a chat turn."
  2. Proposal. "Fanning out to four subagents puts us near 15x."
  3. Return. "We buy coverage across four independent sources and around a 90% cut in wall clock."
  4. Threshold. "Each report drives a decision worth six figures, so the token delta is noise."
  5. Veto check. "Nothing here needs shared state between the branches."

If step 4 is uncomfortable to say, the answer is a single agent. If step 5 fails, the answer is a single agent regardless of how large step 4 is.

SkillFive scenarios, thirty seconds each

Find the binding constraint first. In this lesson it is usually value per task, shared state, or the clock.

A pharmaceutical strategy team needs a competitive landscape for one therapeutic area. The material sits in trial registries, regulatory filings, patent databases and conference abstracts, and no one knows in advance which of them will carry the answer. Each finished landscape feeds an investment decision in the tens of millions. Turnaround expected inside a working day.

Which architecture earns its token bill here?

  1. One lead agent spawning parallel subagents, one per independent source stream.
  2. A single agent working every source in sequence inside one context.
  3. A coded workflow chaining one retrieval pass per already known source.
  4. A chain refining the single draft through five successive critique passes.
Two things made this safe to spend on: the sources are independent and the value per task is enormous. Say the arithmetic out loud and it is not close. Fifteen times a chat turn against a decision worth tens of millions is a rounding error, and the day-long window absorbs the fan-out.

A retail bank triages card disputes as they arrive in the mobile app. Roughly 70% are duplicate charges, 20% are merchant description mismatches and 10% are suspected fraud that always goes to an investigator. The customer must see an outcome status within 3 seconds, and volume runs to 12,000 disputes a day.

Which shape should the architecture take?

  1. Classify the dispute, then route it into small fixed per-category handlers.
  2. A lead agent delegating each dispute check to three parallel subagents.
  3. One agent holding all the dispute tooling and choosing its sequence.
  4. An evaluator critiquing each drafted outcome until a confidence bar clears.
Two constraints both point the same way, which is the tell. High volume kills the multiplier and a tight SLA kills the extra hop. When cost per task and latency both bind, you are being asked to come down the ladder, not go up it. Notice the scenario also enumerated the categories for you.

A logistics platform wants to migrate 300 service files off a deprecated internal client. Every one of those files imports the same shared type definitions, and the migration changes those definitions. The job runs nightly, lands in a reviewable pull request, and there is no deadline pressure at all.

How should the work be executed?

  1. One agent editing sequentially, so each edit sees the previous ones.
  2. Ten subagents in parallel, each owning a disjoint slice of files.
  3. A lead agent replanning the split after every subagent reports back.
  4. Three subagents drafting competing rewrites, with a judge picking one winner.
Anthropic's own line is that "most coding tasks involve fewer truly parallelizable tasks than research". Subagents run in isolated context windows and return only summaries, so parallel editors cannot see each other's changes. A shared file that all of them must modify converts parallelism from a speed-up into a merge problem. No deadline pressure removes the only argument for the risk.

A hospital group is weighing a multi-agent design and wants the justification in writing before it approves the budget.

Which signals genuinely justify paying the multi-agent multiplier?

  1. Independent lines of enquiry that can be pursued at once.
  2. The task is hard and needs careful multi-step reasoning throughout.
  3. The material to read exceeds any single agent's context window.
  4. Every step depends on state the previous step just wrote.
  5. The output has to be reproducible across two identical runs.
Both right answers describe work that genuinely splits. Everything else describes a task being hard, tightly coupled, or needing determinism, and each of those argues down the ladder rather than up it. Option D is the one to hold onto: shared state is the single cleanest veto on multi-agent, and scenarios bury it in a clause about one step using the previous step's output.

A transport authority has shortlisted two bidders for a depot contract and wants a side-by-side on price, delivery record, subcontractor exposure and financial health. The comparison is a direct one between two named organisations, the research budget is real but not tight, and the panel meets on Friday.

How should the lead agent size the work?

  1. Two to four subagents, roughly ten to fifteen tool calls each.
  2. One agent working alone, roughly three to ten tool calls total.
  3. More than ten subagents, each with a clearly divided research brief.
  4. A workflow calling one fixed retrieval prompt for each named bidder.
The rubric is worth committing to memory because it is unusually crisp: 1 agent for lookups, 2 to 4 subagents for comparisons, 10 or more for genuinely open research. Oversizing is a real exam answer, not just an inefficiency. A lead that spawns twenty subagents to compare two bidders is failing the same cost-benefit test as one that refuses to fan out at all.
