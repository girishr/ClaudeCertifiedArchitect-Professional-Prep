Delivery routes and the governance constraint

Three routes to production, and the one you pick decides who counts as a user. Almost every compliance obligation the exam tests hangs off that single classification.

~18 minutes Domain 1, 17% of the exam 5 drills

Lesson 0001 was about picking the right architecture. This one is about the question that gets asked immediately afterwards and that most people answer by instinct: who is on the other end of it. Get the route wrong and a technically correct design collects the wrong obligations, or none at all.

The win by the end of this page: read a scenario, name the route in one sentence, and say which specific governance controls it drags in. That is a lot of exam surface for a short lesson, because the route is upstream of residency, retention, audit and human review all at once.

KnowledgeThree routes, one classification

The routes are not architectures. Any of them can be a workflow or an agent. They describe where the model output ends up, and that is the only thing the compliance questions care about.

The whole lesson in one sentence

Name the route before you name the controls, because the route decides who counts as the user, and every obligation below keys off that one fact.

The governance deltas, concretely

These are the five places the route actually changes your answer. The numbers are worth memorising because the distractors are built from adjacent true facts.

The Usage Policy rule that keeps appearing

The Usage Policy names seven high-risk categories: legal, healthcare, insurance, finance, employment and housing, academic testing and admissions, and media or professional journalistic content. For those, two additional measures are required:

  • Human in the loop. A qualified professional in that field must review the content or decision before it is disseminated or finalised.
  • Disclosure. You must tell people you are using AI to help produce the advice, at minimum at the beginning of each session.

Now read the scope clause, because that is the examinable part. Both measures are written against advice or decisions directly affecting individuals or consumers, and outputs presented directly to individuals or consumers. A pure business-to-business tool, where a trained underwriter reads the output and makes the call, does not trip the disclosure requirement in the same way. Flip that same tool into a customer app and both measures attach immediately.

Why this bites experienced people. Build-time feels ungoverned, because no customer ever sees a model output and there is no disclosure banner to write. The exposure did not disappear, it moved: source code leaving the building, secrets pasted into prompts, transcripts sitting in default 30 day retention, and feedback submissions retained for five years. Different obligations, not fewer. If you run Claude Code across fifty developers, this is your route, and someone will eventually ask you to describe it.

The three-question read

  1. Does the model output reach someone outside your engineering team? If no, it is build-time and the governance surface is your SDLC.
  2. Is that someone a consumer or an individual being decided about? If yes, it is user-facing and the high-risk measures may attach.
  3. Is Claude running inside a production system holding someone else's data? If yes, it is enterprise delivery and residency, retention and audit become contract terms rather than preferences.

SkillFive scenarios, name the route first

In each one, find the sentence that tells you who reads the output. Then answer.

A motor insurer is launching a claims assistant inside its customer app. Policyholders describe the incident in their own words and Claude returns a coverage assessment and an indicative settlement figure, displayed in the app. Legal has approved the model. Nobody has written the operating rules yet.

What does Anthropic's Usage Policy require before this ships?

  1. A qualified professional reviews each assessment, and the app discloses AI use.
  2. A zero data retention agreement covering every inference the claims assistant makes.
  3. An audit log capturing every prompt and response for seven complete years.
  4. A workspace geo pinned so inference never leaves the insurer's home country.
Insurance claims processing is a named high-risk category, and the output goes straight to the policyholder. That combination is the only one that fires both measures. Note what the policy does not say: it sets no retention period, no log format and no residency rule. Those are real controls that belong to other questions.

A retailer's platform team is running Claude Code to migrate 900 services off a deprecated payments SDK. Generated code goes through the normal pull request process before release. No shopper ever sees a model output. The security team asks which compliance work the project inherits.

Which route is this, and where does the governance work actually land?

  1. Build-time, so governance lands on code review, secret handling and transcript retention.
  2. User-facing, so governance lands on AI disclosure, review gates and consumer redress.
  3. Enterprise delivery, so governance lands on tenant isolation, uptime targets and residency.
  4. Build-time, so no governance lands anywhere beyond the existing normal release process.
Build-time is the route people forget to govern, because the absence of an end user reads as the absence of risk. The right answer is not "nothing applies", it is "different things apply". Under commercial terms Anthropic does not train on your code, but retention, telemetry and feedback submissions are still yours to configure.

A German bank will embed Claude in its internal credit memo system. The regulator's stated condition is that inference must run inside the EU and be provable per request. Procurement wants a yes or no this week so the contract can be signed.

What is the honest answer to give the regulator today?

  1. No. Inference geo currently offers only us and global, so EU-only is unavailable.
  2. Yes. Set inference_geo to eu per request and read the confirmation field back.
  3. Yes. Create the workspace with an EU workspace geo, which pins everything permanently.
  4. Yes. Enable zero data retention, so nothing persists anywhere and residency turns moot.
Two things to carry. First, residency and retention are different controls and answering one with the other is the classic trap. Second, the consulting half of the exam rewards the architect who says "that constraint cannot be met with this control today" rather than the one who invents a parameter value. Escalate to the account team, do not sign.

A hospital group operates under a zero data retention arrangement. It wants to summarise 200,000 discharge notes overnight. An engineer proposes the Batch API to collect the 50% discount, reasoning that the job is asynchronous anyway and cost is the only open question.

What is wrong with the proposal?

  1. Batch processing is not ZDR eligible and carries twenty-nine day result retention.
  2. Batch throughput cannot reach two hundred thousand requests in one overnight window.
  3. Batch discounts never stack with prompt caching, so the saving disappears entirely.
  4. Batch results expire completely after one hour, too short for overnight jobs.
ZDR is a feature-scoped promise, not an account-wide one. Batch, the Files API, code execution and managed agents all sit outside it, each for the same structural reason: they need to store something to work at all. When a scenario pairs a retention commitment with a convenience feature, check the eligibility table before you check the price.

A council piloted Claude internally, with caseworkers drafting housing benefit decision letters from model output. It now wants residents to query their own eligibility in a self-service portal, using the same model and the same prompts, and to drop the caseworker step so the projected savings actually materialise.

Which two requirements does the Usage Policy impose on the new design?

  1. Disclosure of AI involvement at the beginning of every resident session.
  2. Qualified professional review before any eligibility answer is shown to residents.
  3. A zero data retention arrangement covering every call the portal makes.
  4. A different model tier, because public traffic demands deeper reasoning capacity.
  5. Immutable workspace geo pinning, because residents are individuals rather than staff.
The council's plan is the exact move the policy is written to stop: dropping human review at the moment the output starts reaching individuals. Notice that the model, the prompts and the architecture are unchanged. Only the route flipped, and the obligations arrived with it. That is the whole skill this lesson is training.

SourceRead this next

Primary source: Anthropic Usage Policy, the High-Risk Use Case Requirements section specifically. Ten minutes, and it is short enough to read in full. Read it for the exact wording of "individuals or consumers", because that phrase is doing all the work: it is what separates a regulated consumer product from a B2B tool that happens to operate in the same industry, and the exam builds distractors out of exactly that gap.

Then, when you have twenty more minutes, the API and data retention feature eligibility table. It is a lookup table, not an argument, and it is the densest page of examinable facts in this domain.
