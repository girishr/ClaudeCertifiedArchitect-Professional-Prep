The six patterns, and their traps

Everyone learns the six shapes in an afternoon. The exam tests the preconditions, which is where a pattern that looks right stops being allowed.

~20 minutes Domain 1, 17% of the exam 5 drills

Reference architectures are the easy half of Domain 1. You can name all six from Building effective agents already. The hard half is that most questions give you two patterns whose shape fits, and the separation is a precondition sitting in one clause of the scenario: how accurate the classifier is, whether anyone can write down what good looks like, whether the subtask list exists before runtime.

The win by the end of this page: for any scenario you name the pattern and, in the same breath, the precondition you just checked. That second half is what turns a guess into an answer you can defend.

KnowledgeShape suggests it, precondition permits it

Anthropic frames these as building blocks rather than a taxonomy: "these building blocks aren't prescriptive. They're common patterns that developers can shape and combine to fit different use cases." Read the middle column as what the scenario looks like, and the third as the thing that has to be true before you are allowed to say it.

The whole lesson in one sentence

The shape of the scenario suggests a pattern; the precondition permits it, and a distractor is usually a pattern whose shape fits and whose precondition fails.

Sectioning versus orchestrator-workers

Lesson 0001 flagged this as the domain's favourite trap, so it is worth stating in Anthropic's own words. The difference from parallelisation is "flexibility: subtasks aren't pre-defined, but determined by the orchestrator based on the specific input".

Both fan out. Both look identical on a whiteboard. The whole distinction is who decides the split and when. In sectioning you decide it, in code, before anything runs, so cost and latency are known before the first token. In orchestrator-workers a lead model decides it at runtime from the input, which is the only reason to pay for the planning hop.

The scenario tells you which one every time, usually in a single clause. "Every contract gets the same four checks" is sectioning. "Which analyses are needed depends on what is in the folder" is the orchestrator. If a scenario enumerates the subtasks for you, it has already ruled the orchestrator out.

The price matters too. Anthropic's multi-agent research system measured agent interactions at roughly 4x the tokens of a chat exchange and multi-agent systems at about 15x, which is only worth it when value per task is high. It bought a 90.2% improvement over single-agent Opus on a breadth-first research eval, and the same post is blunt that most coding work is a poor fit, because the subtasks are interdependent and agents cannot see each other's edits.

Why this bites experienced people. If you have built orchestration before, fan-out reads as orchestration by reflex, and a planner feels like good engineering rather than a line item. Under exam time pressure the fastest correction is mechanical: before you answer orchestrator-workers, find the sentence that says the subtasks vary. If you cannot point at it, it is sectioning.

Two preconditions that do most of the eliminating

Routing needs accurate classification. The stated condition is "distinct categories that are better handled separately, and where classification can be handled accurately, either by an LLM or a more traditional classification model or algorithm". This one is unforgiving because the error is silent and upstream. A misrouted input gets a confident, well-formatted answer to the wrong question, and nothing downstream can rescue it. If a scenario gives you a classifier accuracy number, that number is the answer.

Evaluator-optimizer needs articulable criteria. It is "particularly effective when we have clear evaluation criteria, and when iterative refinement provides measurable value", with two signs of fit: "that LLM responses can be demonstrably improved when a human articulates their feedback; and second, that the LLM can provide such feedback". Both, not either. A team that cannot agree what good looks like does not get a critique loop, they get a definition exercise. And when the criteria are code-checkable, a validator is cheaper than a critic.

SkillFive scenarios, name the precondition too

Read the last sentence first, find the clause that eliminates options, then answer. Say the precondition out loud before you click.

A streaming platform screens user-uploaded clips against a short list of prohibited content before publication. A miss reaches millions of viewers and draws a regulator letter; a false alarm costs a moderator two minutes. Volume is 40,000 clips a day and the per-clip budget is generous.

Which pattern fits?

  1. Chain three refinement passes so each verdict reaches its highest accuracy.
  2. Route each clip to a specialised prompt chosen by cheap classification.
  3. Run several independent checks per clip and escalate any single flag.
  4. Let an autonomous agent decide which checks each individual clip warrants.
Voting is the one pattern whose precondition is economic rather than structural: a miss must cost more than the extra calls. Here the asymmetry is stated twice, in the regulator letter and in the two wasted minutes, and the budget line removes the usual objection. Flip the cost asymmetry and voting becomes an expensive way to be equally right.

A commercial insurer underwrites bespoke policies. Each submission arrives as a folder of documents, and which analyses are needed depends on what is in the folder. The underwriters cannot list them in advance. Turnaround is measured in days and every step is logged to the policy file.

Which pattern fits?

  1. Sectioning, splitting the folder into four fixed analyses run concurrently.
  2. Orchestrator-workers, letting a lead model decide the analyses per submission.
  3. Routing, dispatching each submission to one of four underwriting prompts.
  4. Evaluator-optimizer, critiquing each draft analysis until a reviewer accepts it.
"Cannot list them in advance" is the clause, and it is the only one that matters. Days of turnaround is what makes the answer affordable, since the planner hop and the variable token bill have somewhere to hide. Put this same submission under a two second SLA and the correct answer changes to a fixed split that covers the common case.

A government helpline wants routing across eleven benefit schemes. A pilot on 2,000 real calls put classifier accuracy at 71%, and a misrouted caller receives a confident answer about the wrong scheme, which they then act on. Budget allows one significant change before launch.

What should you do first?

  1. Ship routing anyway and monitor misroute rates closely after go-live.
  2. Add an evaluator-optimizer loop critiquing each answer before it goes.
  3. Replace routing with an autonomous agent holding eleven scheme tools.
  4. Fix classification first, or serve one prompt covering eleven schemes.
When a scenario hands you an accuracy figure, it is not colour, it is the question. Routing's error mode is silent and upstream, so the damage is done before any downstream component sees the work. The consulting instinct is the same as the architectural one here: fix the input to the pattern, or drop the pattern and take the small quality loss of one general prompt.

A retailer generates product descriptions inside the page render path, with a 400ms end-to-end budget. A single call passes the quality eval at 94% and the content team wants 97%. Someone has proposed a three-step draft, critique and rewrite chain to close the gap.

What do you tell them?

  1. Keep the existing single call, running the chain nightly for backlog.
  2. Adopt the chain, since three percentage points of quality justify latency.
  3. Adopt voting, running three parallel calls and picking the best description.
  4. Adopt an orchestrator that plans the description steps for each product.
Chaining's whole proposition is that it "trades off latency for higher accuracy", so a hard interactive budget rules it out by definition, not by judgement. The move is to keep the pattern and change where it runs. Precomputed overnight, the same chain buys the three points with no latency cost at all, which is the answer the content team actually wanted.

A bank drafts credit memos with Claude and someone has proposed wrapping the drafting step in an evaluator-optimizer loop. Memos are produced overnight, so extra rounds cost nothing in wall clock, and the credit team is split on whether the current drafts are actually weak.

Which two facts would justify building the loop?

  1. The drafting model is already the largest, most expensive tier.
  2. Reviewers can articulate, in writing, what makes a memo good.
  3. The overnight window has spare capacity for several extra rounds.
  4. A critic model reproduces those judgements on held-out test memos.
  5. Three reviewers each defined quality differently when asked last year.
Anthropic names both signs and they are conjunctive: a human can articulate the feedback, and the model can provide it. Option C is the seductive one, because latency headroom is usually the blocker and its absence feels like permission. Cheap is not the same as valuable. Option E is the state the bank is actually in, and it points at a definition exercise rather than an architecture.
