CCAR-P course / Module 2 / Section 12 of 12

Six takeaways, three per domain. Notice that five of the six are about restraint: pick the weakest coupling that works, consolidate before you scale, refuse to build retrieval you do not need, grade with the cheapest instrument that can see the property, and fix at the cheapest layer that reaches the failure.

The six takeaways
#TakeawayThe operative detail
01The constraint in the scenario picks the mechanismWhere the capability lives, who else consumes it, and what is not allowed to move. Those three sentences eliminate three of four options before cost or elegance enters. Data must stay on the device means stdio; many hosts plus central audit means remote; resources or prompts means a real client, not the connector
02Tool descriptions and error strings are the two highest-value things you controlBefore the system prompt, before the model tier. Consolidate around workflows rather than endpoints, bound the response, resolve UUIDs to names, and put the actionable sentence in the isError: true result. Past 30 to 50 tools, defer loading and add search
03Every token belongs to one server and every tool result is untrustedAudience validation, not signature validation. Never forward a token you were given. Per-client consent before forwarding when a proxy holds a static client ID. A result flowing from one server into another is still attacker-controlled text
04An eval number only means something if the distribution and the sample size holdMirror production then add edge cases, keep a holdout you never tune against, and prefer many auto-graded cases to few hand-graded ones. Two points on fifty cases is not a result
05Pick the metric that moves when the thing you care about breaksTTFT for anything a human watches, cost per task for agents, end-state as the primary agent signal with trajectory as the diagnostic. Criteria are SMAR and usually multidimensional
06Name the failure before naming the fix, then take the cheapest lever that reaches itTranscript, context presence, tool errors, hallucination, prompt, capability. Then prompt, tools, few-shot, caching, context reduction, routing, batch, and fine-tuning-like last. Pin the model version so the failure has somewhere to be traced to

12.2Common distractors, Domain 4

Twelve wrong answers, each a real technique in the wrong place
The optionWhy it is wrong
"Fine-tune the model"Almost never the first move
"Add more few-shot examples", offered against a retrieval miss or a tool errorWrong layer
"Use an LLM judge for a classification eval"Slower, noisier and less reliable than exact match
"Have the model grade its own output"With no rubric and no human calibration, that is not a measurement
"Hand-grade 1,000 items"Right instinct, wrong economics. Automate grading, spend humans on calibration
"Ship to 100% and monitor"Canary and shadow exist to avoid exactly this
"A/B test it"Wrong when traffic cannot reach significance, or when a shadow run would answer it with no user risk
"Grade against the golden trajectory"Over-strict whenever multiple valid paths exist
"Measure tokens per second" as the user-facing latency metric for chatTTFT is what the user feels
"Enable prompt caching" to fix accuracyCaching changes cost and TTFT, not quality
"Use batch processing" for anything interactiveNo streaming, and up to 24 hours
"Raise temperature" when the complaint is inconsistencyThat makes it worse

12.3Closed-book self-test

No scrolling. Twelve items, drawn from both domains. Anything you cannot produce cleanly is the section to reread, not the whole module.

  1. How many MCP clients does a host create, what are the two transports, and which party controls tools, resources and prompts respectively?
  2. Name four things deprecated in the 2026-07-28 spec, and the one client-facing feature that survived. What replaced protocol-level session state?
  3. What are the two error channels in tool calling, which one can the model act on, and what belongs in it?
  4. Give the three bloat numbers: where selection accuracy starts to degrade, the context reduction tool search reports, and the threshold at which progressive discovery is worth building.
  5. What must a server validate about an incoming token, and what are the four conditions that together create the confused deputy?
  6. Recite the contextual retrieval reductions for the three configurations against baseline, and the corpus size below which you should not build RAG at all.
  7. Which telemetry is on by default and which is opt-in, and what is the answer to "we need traces without exporting customer PII"?
  8. Rank the four grading methods on reliability and nuance, and give the four-part LLM-judge recipe plus three named judge biases.
  9. What is the primary metric for an agent, what two metrics guard it, and why is cost per task rather than cost per request the right unit?
  10. Contrast shadow and canary in one sentence each, and give the Batch API's discount, typical completion, ceiling and size limits.
  11. Walk the six steps of the diagnosis order, then name the discriminator for a prompt problem, a retrieval problem, a context problem and a capability problem.
  12. List the optimisation levers in order, cheapest first, and give the prompt-cache prefix order plus the three cache multipliers.

12.4What this buys on the exam

Module 2 maps to Domain 3, Integration (19 percent), and Domain 4, Evaluation, Testing & Optimization (16 percent). That is 35 percent of the paper, the heaviest of the five modules, and roughly 22 questions on a 63-question exam. Five or six of them will be multiple-response, clustered in Domain 4.

The two domains reward different revision. Domain 3 rewards recall of exact rules: the MUST and MUST NOT language in section 5, the deprecations in section 3, the thresholds in section 4. Domain 4 rewards the two ordered lists in section 11 and nothing else nearly as much. If you have one evening left before the paper, spend it on section 5 and section 11.
