Eval sets and grading methods

What goes in the golden set, how big it has to be to mean anything, and which grader each output shape earns.

Exam domain 4From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 2 / Section 08 of 12

Every Domain 4 question depends on one prior decision: whether the number you are about to act on means anything. Two things break that, and both are design errors rather than measurement errors. The eval's distribution does not match production, or the sample is too small to see the difference you care about. Fix those and most of the rest of this domain is choosing the cheapest grader that can see the thing you are grading.

8.1Building the set

Seven properties of an eval set you can act on
PropertyWhat it means in practice
Mirrors the real task distributionThen deliberately add edge cases on top. An eval whose distribution differs from production tells you nothing about production, and no amount of rigour downstream repairs that
Volume beats per-item polishAnthropic's stated preference is many questions with slightly noisier automated grading over a few hand-graded gems. Use Claude to expand a hand-written seed set
A golden datasetInputs plus verified expected outputs or acceptance criteria. Version-controlled, owned, with provenance. Every production bug becomes a new case
A holdout splitTune on one split, validate on a split you never iterate against. Anthropic says this explicitly for agent-assisted tool refinement, because otherwise you overfit descriptions to your own eval
Sized for the difference you care aboutAt roughly 90% accuracy, tens of cases give about plus or minus 10 points. Detecting a few-point regression needs a few hundred. If two variants differ by 2 points on 50 cases, you have measured nothing
Verifiable without being over-strictPair each prompt with a verifiable outcome, but do not fail valid alternative phrasings or formatting
Realistic and multi-step for agentsSchedule the meeting, attach the doc, book the room. Not single lookups
Watch out

The sample-size point is the one that shows up disguised as a reasonable conclusion. A scenario reports variant B beating variant A by two points on fifty cases and asks what to do next. The answer is not "ship B". It is that the run cannot distinguish them.

8.2Four grading methods

Pick by the shape of the output, not by how thorough the method sounds. The last column is the one to read first.

Grading methods on four axes
MethodSpeedReliabilityScaleNuanceUse when
Code or exact matchFastestHighestHighestNoneClassification, extraction, schema conformance, tool-argument checks
Fuzzy or structural (regex, contains, embedding similarity)FastMediumHighLowRequired facts present, format checks
LLM-as-judgeFastGood if validatedHighHighOpen-ended text, summaries, tone, multi-criteria rubrics
Human reviewSlowHighPoorHighestCalibrating the judge, safety sign-off, ambiguous new domains

Humans are not the premium option you buy when budget allows. They are the calibration instrument. Spend them on validating the judge and on safety sign-off, and automate the volume.

8.3LLM-judge discipline

This is where the pitfall questions live, because an unvalidated judge is an opinion with a p-value. Six rules:

  1. A detailed rubric with hard rules. "Must mention Acme Inc. in the first sentence, otherwise incorrect."
  2. Force discrete output. Correct or incorrect, or a 1 to 5 scale. Never free-form praise.
  3. Reason first, then score, and discard the reasoning.
  4. Validate against human labels before you trust it.
  5. Name the biases: position bias in pairwise comparisons, which you fix by randomising order; verbosity bias; self-preference; and leniency drift as rubrics get vaguer.
  6. Never use the same model and prompt as both generator and sole judge for a gate you will act on.

A support-triage system returns two things per ticket: one of 14 category labels, and a one-sentence justification that must name the policy clause it relied on. The team wants a nightly regression suite over 800 saved tickets, running in CI, on a small budget. They have about two hours of a domain expert's time per week.

Select TWO grading methods for the nightly run.

  1. Exact match on the category label
  2. Human review of all 800 justifications each night
  3. An LLM judge on the justification, against a rubric, validated against human labels first
  4. Embedding similarity between the whole output and a reference answer
Two output shapes, two graders. A closed label set never needs a judge, and an open sentence with a hard rule in it cannot be graded by string comparison. The scarce human time is the detail that tells you where the calibration happens: on the judge, once, not on 800 items a night.

So the rule to carry: grade with the cheapest method that can actually see the property you care about, and let the output shape decide, one property at a time.
