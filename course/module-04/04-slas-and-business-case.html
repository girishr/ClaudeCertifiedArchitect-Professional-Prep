SLAs and the business case

What the unit of cost is, what the latency budget is spent on, what you may sign, and when to buy instead of build.

Exam domain 6From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 4 / Section 04 of 8

Section 3 set the expectation. This section turns it into numbers somebody signs, and the signing is where a careless sentence becomes a liability. There are three ways to get it wrong: quote a cost per task that omits half the cost, allocate a latency budget after the build rather than before it, and commit contractually to something no eval can defend.

4.1Cost per task, the unit that travels

Cost per task is the number that survives translation between engineering and finance, so it is the number to get right. Six components, and teams routinely count three.

What goes into one task
ComponentNote
Input tokensIncluding the system prompt, retrieved context and few-shot examples, on every call
Output tokensPriced higher than input on every route
RetriesA validation failure or a refusal that triggers a second attempt costs a second task
Guardrail and classifier callsRoutinely forgotten. A pre-filter and a post-check are two more model calls per task
RetrievalEmbedding, vector search, reranking where used
Human review minutesRoutinely forgotten, and usually the largest line. A review gate is a labour cost per task, and it is the cost that decides whether the ROI is real

The two bold rows are the reason teams miss their cost target having done the token arithmetic correctly. If a stem says cost per task is over budget, the first places to look are model routing, caching, prompt size and review minutes, in that order, before anyone declares the use case infeasible.

4.2The latency budget is allocated, not discovered

Split the end-to-end budget across retrieval, model, validation and network up front, and hold each stage to its slice. A budget discovered after the build is a series of arguments about whose component is at fault.

Levers when the budget is tight
LeverWhat it actually buys
A smaller model for sub-stepsReal time, on classification, routing and extraction, where the cheap model is not measurably worse
Prompt cachingReal time and real money on a stable prefix. Free win where the system prompt and corpus dominate the input
StreamingPerceived latency only. Total time to completion is unchanged, and that is the honest way to describe it. Still often the right answer for a user-facing surface
Validation off the critical pathReal time, where safe. Not available when the validation is the control preventing a harmful output from reaching a user

4.3What an SLA may commit to

This is the highest-consequence table in the module. An SLA written for deterministic software is the classic wrong commitment, and it is wrong on a specific line rather than in spirit.

The line the commitment must not cross
Commit toShape it takesNever commit to
AvailabilityAn uptime percentage, measured the way any service is measured. Deterministic, monitorable, defensibleA contractual accuracy percentage on open-ended generation. It is not measurable in a way that survives a dispute, the output space is unbounded, and you have converted every quality disagreement into a breach of contract
Latency percentilesp50, p95 and p99, each with a number. Percentiles rather than an average, because the average hides the experience that generates the complaint
A process for qualityA named eval set, a monthly review of results against it, and a defined remediation path when the number moves. You are committing to the machinery that keeps quality honest, which is a commitment you can keep

Note what the third row does. It is not a refusal to talk about quality. It is a substitution of something contractible for something that is not, and the client gets more from it: a review cadence and a remediation path are worth more than a percentage nobody can adjudicate.

A legal services firm is contracting you to build an assistant that drafts first-pass summaries of incoming case correspondence for paralegals, who edit before anything is filed. Procurement will not sign without a quality clause, and the general counsel has said plainly that a services agreement in this firm always carries a measurable performance commitment. Your pilot measured 91 percent on a 400-document set against a human baseline of 88 percent, and the sponsor is happy to write 90 percent into the contract to get it signed this quarter.

What do you put in the SLA?

  1. 90 percent accuracy on the named 400-document eval set, since the pilot already exceeded it and the set is specified in the contract
  2. Availability, p50, p95 and p99 latency, plus a quality process: the named eval set, a monthly review against it, and a defined remediation path if results move
  3. 90 percent accuracy, with liability capped at the monthly fee and a carve-out for atypical documents
  4. No quality commitment until after six months in production, when there is enough data to know what is safe to promise
B, and the deciding word in the stem is "drafts". The work is open-ended generation, which is what rules out a contractual accuracy percentage however good the pilot number was and however specific the eval set is. Commit to availability, to latency percentiles, and to the process that keeps quality measured. The 91 percent still belongs in the conversation, as a target on a named set with a fallback, which is section 3's sentence and not a contract term.

4.4ROI, discounted for the ramp

The formula is unglamorous and you should be able to write it out:

(baseline cost - new cost - run cost) x volume, minus build cost, over a stated horizon

Three things people get wrong. Run cost is not build cost, and a business case that shows only build cost is understating the total. The horizon must be stated, because the same project is a triumph over three years and a loss over one. And the saving must be discounted by the adoption ramp: a 40 percent saving achieved at 30 percent adoption is a 12 percent saving. Quote the 40 percent alone and you will be held to the 40 percent.

4.5The pilot-to-production gates

Eight gates. All eight, not most of them, and they are worth memorising as a list because items are written by removing one and seeing whether you notice.

  1. Eval threshold met on held-out data
  2. Cost inside budget, measured as cost per task
  3. Latency inside budget, at the committed percentiles
  4. Guardrail incidents at or below the agreed threshold
  5. A named owner for the system after go-live
  6. Runbook complete
  7. Rollback tested, not merely documented
  8. Security and compliance signed off
Note the shape of that list. Two are quality, two are performance, and four are operational. The operational half is what distinguishes a pilot from a product, and it is the half an enthusiastic team skips because the demo already works.

4.6Build against buy

Which side of the line the capability sits on
Buy whenBuild when
The capability is undifferentiated and a vendor's roadmap will outrun yours. You will never out-invest a company whose only product this is The workflow is your differentiator. Buying it means buying your competitor's workflow with your logo on it
Time to value matters more than fit, and the vendor is close enough on fit The data cannot leave your boundary, which is a constraint rather than a preference and settles the question on its own
The cost of running it yourself, including the people, exceeds the licence Integration depth is the value. If the point is that it reaches into six internal systems, the integration is the product
Watch out

When a stem pairs a tight timeline with a proprietary workflow, the answer is usually hybrid: buy the platform, build the domain logic on top of it. Both pure options are wrong for a stated reason. Building everything misses the date; buying everything discards the differentiator you were hired to protect. The hybrid option is easy to skip in a list because it reads as a compromise, and here it is the engineering answer.

Leave this section able to state a cost per task with all six components in it, a latency budget with four named slices, and an SLA that commits availability and percentiles and a quality process, and nothing else.
