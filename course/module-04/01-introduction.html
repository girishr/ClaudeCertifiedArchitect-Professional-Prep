The half that decides whether it ships

A technically correct architecture that nobody adopts has failed, and this is the domain that decides which it will be.

Exam domain 6From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 4 / Section 01 of 8

Three modules of work can produce a design that is right in every particular and still never reach a user. The pattern was correct, the controls were proportionate, the evals passed, and the sponsor quietly stopped funding it because the pilot had no agreed success criteria and the number that came back was not the number he had in his head. That project failed. Not partially: a system nobody adopts delivers nothing, and the architecture is what it delivers.

So treat this domain as engineering, because that is how it is examined. It is not tested as soft skills or stakeholder charm. It is tested as judgement about people and money under uncertainty, and the wrong answers are as precise and as technically literate as the wrong answers anywhere else on the paper.

Watch out

This is the domain most likely to be underestimated, and underestimating it is structural rather than careless. Everything in it looks like something you already do. The gap is that you do it by feel, and the exam tests it as a set of rules with names: which metric belongs to which stakeholder, what may appear in an SLA, which stage of the lifecycle you are standing in, and what you say first when the news is bad.

1.1What the exam is actually testing

Picture the room the items are written from. A sponsor who wants a guarantee. A compliance lead who wants a veto. An engineering lead who wants a spec. And a pilot sitting at 78 percent against a 90 percent target, with three weeks left in the budget cycle.

Against that, the examiner rewards answers that make uncertainty explicit and agree a decision rule in advance. It punishes three things specifically: promising determinism, over-engineering before value is proven, and escalating before diagnosing.

That gives you a tiebreaker worth memorising, because this domain's options are unusually close. When every option looks like something a competent consultant might reasonably do, pick the one that either reduces ambiguity about success criteria or surfaces bad news early with a recommendation attached. One of those two is very often the key.

1.2The trap this domain sets

Most domains build distractors from real techniques used at the wrong time. This one builds them from something harder to resist: the most accommodating thing you could say to the person in front of you.

Every wrong answer below is what a stakeholder would most like to hear in the moment. That is what makes them work as distractors, and the defence is to price the accommodation. Ask what it costs when the bill arrives.

The accommodating answer, and what it costs later
What the stakeholder wants to hearWhat it costs when the bill arrives
"Yes, we can commit to 99 percent accuracy in the contract"You have signed a guarantee on open-ended generation that no eval can defend. The first disputed output is a contractual breach rather than a quality conversation
"Let's start with the highest-value use case"High value with low feasibility is the quadrant where pilots die. You spend the sponsor's goodwill on the hardest problem and have nothing to show at the end of it
"We'll give you another quarter to get there"Time is not an intervention. Nothing about the hypothesis, the scope or the measurement changed, so the same number arrives a quarter later with less credibility behind it
"Fine, let's call 80 percent the target"The target moved and nobody recorded that it moved or why. Every later conversation about performance is now unanchored, and you own the ambiguity
"We'll run more training sessions to lift adoption"If the new workflow is worse for the user, training teaches them faster access to something they do not want. The workflow, not the knowledge, is the defect
"Let me take this to the steering committee"Escalation before diagnosis reads as abdication. You have spent the committee's attention without a recommendation to spend it on

None of those is stupid. Each is what a reasonable person says under pressure, which is exactly why they are on the paper.

Six moves, in the order the engagement makes them.

Weight. Domain 6 is 14 percent of the paper, roughly nine questions of 63, and the official module is the second longest in the path at 178 minutes. Read that as a deliberate weighting rather than generosity: Anthropic is treating the engagement as part of the architecture.

The lab that pairs with this module is Lab 8, a timeboxed run at the four artefacts this domain is examined on: a discovery interview you have to actually conduct, an architecture decision record, a phased rollout plan with kill criteria, and an executive one-pager that survives a hostile reader. Reading the formats is worth something; writing one of each is worth considerably more, because what the exam tests is format discipline under a stem's time pressure.

Work through the module asking one question of every scenario: what has been agreed, in writing, with whom. Most of the failures in this domain are failures of an unwritten assumption, and most of the correct answers are the act of writing one down.
