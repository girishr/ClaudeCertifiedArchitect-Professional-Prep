Lifecycle and rollout

Three stages that make three different promises, a ramp that retires one unknown at a time, and why adoption fails when accuracy is fine.

Exam domain 6From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 4 / Section 06 of 8

The demo worked on eight examples, the room applauded, and somebody asked how quickly it could go live. That moment is a named exam scenario and the answer is always the same: a POC promoted straight to production is wrong, because the only stage that measures whether anyone gets value from it has been skipped.

POC, pilot and production are not three sizes of the same thing. They make three different promises and they earn three different levels of investment.

Three stages, three promises
StageWhat it provesWhat it requiresOutput
POC That the task is technically possible A handful of examples. No production constraints, no real users, no integration A yes or a no. Nothing else
Pilot That there is value, with real users, real data, real constraints and a measured baseline Pre-agreed acceptance criteria, an eval set, monitoring, and an end date A number against an agreed threshold, and a decision
Production That it will keep working under load, drift and staff turnover SLOs, on-call, rollback, security review, cost controls, a named owner. The eight gates in section 4 A system somebody runs

The end date on the pilot is load-bearing. A pilot without one becomes a permanent state in which nobody has to decide anything, and "extend the pilot" becomes the path of least resistance every quarter.

6.1The phased ramp, one unknown at a time

Each phase exists to retire a specific unknown, and a phase that does not name its unknown is just a delay. Give every phase an exit criterion and a kill criterion, because a phase you cannot fail tells you nothing, and name the person who is allowed to stop the rollout.

Four phases, and what each one is for
PhaseWho sees itThe unknown it retiresWhat must be in place
1 Internal dogfood Your own team Does it survive contact with people who did not build it, on inputs nobody curated? Somewhere to log every surprise. The worst outcome at this phase is embarrassment, which is what makes it the cheapest place to learn
2 Friendly cohort A small set of real users who will tolerate a rough edge and report it Does it hold on live traffic, and do users act on the output? A visible feedback path and someone answering it. A cohort chosen for goodwill, not for volume
3 Percentage ramp with canary A growing share of the population Does it hold at volume and at cost, on a wider input distribution? Automatic rollback triggers, armed before the ramp begins. A canary nobody is watching is not a canary
4 GA Everyone Nothing. By here the unknowns should be retired The runbook, the owner and the on-call rotation actually in use rather than written down
Watch out

"The pilot went well, so roll it out to everyone" is the accommodating answer at this stage and it is wrong for a structural reason rather than a cautious one: pilot distribution rarely survives the full population. The pilot cohort volunteered, which means they were more motivated, more tolerant and working on more typical inputs than the average. What it costs is the one thing you cannot buy back, which is first impressions across an entire organisation. A failure at 5 percent is a rollback; the same failure at 100 percent is the story people tell about AI at that company for two years.

Next

Lab 8, step 4, is a four-phase plan where every phase names the unknown it retires, an exit criterion, a kill criterion, a rollback plan and a named person who can stop it. It also asks you to state what you will not measure, which is what stops a success story being retro-fitted to whatever happened.

6.2Change management is not training

When adoption is low and accuracy is fine, the model is not the problem. In exam stems, adoption failures come from workflow disruption or lack of trust, and rarely from missing features. Six moves, none of which is a training session.

What actually moves adoption
MoveWhy it works
Identify who loses time or statusEvery deployment has someone whose job got worse or whose expertise got commoditised. Unnamed, that person becomes quiet, effective resistance
Name the "what's in it for me", per roleThe organisational benefit is not a personal benefit. Cycle time down 40 percent can read to a reviewer as more work arriving faster
Recruit champions per teamAdoption travels between peers, not down from a programme. One credible colleague per team beats any amount of central communication
Train on the new workflow, not the toolPeople do not need to be taught the interface. They need to know what their job looks like on Monday
Publish what the system cannot doUsers calibrate on the first failure. If you named it in advance, the failure confirms your credibility; if you did not, it destroys it
Give a visible feedback path, with evidence that feedback changed somethingA feedback form nobody hears back from is worse than none. The evidence, not the channel, is what buys trust
Watch out

"Run more training sessions" is the distractor whenever the stem has said the workflow itself got worse for the people using it. It is attractive because it is an action, it is cheap, and it puts the burden on the users rather than on the design. What it costs is a second failed rollout: you have taught people to use something faster that they were rejecting on the merits, and you have spent the goodwill that would have funded the workflow fix.

6.3The post-launch loop

Go-live is where the lifecycle starts repeating, and a system without this loop degrades quietly.

  • Feedback signals land in a triage queue that somebody owns.
  • Every failure case becomes an eval case. This is the single habit that compounds: the suite gets stronger exactly where the system is weak, and a regression you have seen once you never ship twice.
  • A monthly eval review, which is also the review the SLA's quality process committed to in section 4.
  • Quarterly re-baselining against newer models, because the cheapest performance improvement available is usually a model that shipped after you built.
  • Drift monitoring on the input distribution and the refusal rate. A rising refusal rate is often the first visible sign that the inputs have moved.

Match the investment to the stage and keep the ability to stop. A rollout you cannot halt and a pilot you cannot fail are the same mistake wearing different clothes.
