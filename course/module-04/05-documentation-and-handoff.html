Documentation and handoff

The seven artefacts an implementation team needs to be able to change the system without breaking it.

Exam domain 6From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 4 / Section 05 of 8

A handoff has one test and it is not whether documents exist. It is whether a team that was not in the room can change the system safely: adjust a prompt, swap a model, widen the scope, and know within an hour whether they have made it worse.

Most handoffs fail that test while looking complete, because the documents produced are the ones that were easy to write. What follows is the priority order, which is also roughly the order in which the missing one hurts.

5.1The seven artefacts

What implementation teams actually need, in rough priority order
ArtefactContainsWhy it matters
Architecture decision recordsDecision, context, options, consequences, date, ownerStops the team relitigating settled questions, and explains why the obvious alternative was rejected. Without it, the obvious alternative gets rebuilt in six months
Prompt artefactsVersioned prompts, rationale, few-shot sets, changelogPrompts are code. Unversioned prompts are the top handoff failure
Eval suiteGolden dataset, scoring method, thresholds, CI integrationWithout it nobody can safely change anything. This is the artefact that makes the system maintainable rather than frozen
RunbookFailure modes, alerts, kill switch, rollback steps, escalation contacts, on-callThe difference between a pilot and a product
Guardrail and permission specWhich layer catches what, tool scopes, approval matrixAudit evidence, and the fastest way to onboard an engineer into what the system is allowed to do
Cost and capacity modelToken math, rate limits, scaling assumptionsPrevents the surprise invoice, which is the fastest way to lose a sponsor who was otherwise happy
Known limitationsWhat it cannot do, out-of-scope inputs, disclosure textSets user expectations and protects the team. Publishing the limits is what buys trust, not what costs it
Watch out

"Hand over the prompts in a shared doc" is the distractor, and it is tempting because it is fast, cooperative and visibly generous: the receiving team gets everything today rather than after a week of tooling. What it costs is the ability to diagnose. Three months later the output has changed, nobody knows which edit did it, there is no changelog and no version to roll back to, and the only remaining move is to rewrite the prompt from scratch and re-earn the confidence the pilot had already bought. Prompts are versioned artefacts with a changelog, and they arrive with the eval suite that scores them.

The pairing to remember. Prompt artefacts and the eval suite are one deliverable in two files. Versioned prompts without evals give you a history you cannot evaluate. Evals without versioned prompts tell you something broke and not what.

5.2The decision record, and the two sections people skip

An ADR is short and its value is concentrated in the parts that are least satisfying to write. A decision record that lists only the decision is a decision announcement, and exam items about documentation quality turn on exactly that distinction.

Which sections carry the weight
SectionWhat it must contain
StatusProposed, accepted, superseded. One word, and it stops people acting on a draft
ContextThe baselines and constraints from discovery, with numbers, and an explicit note of what you do not yet know
DecisionThe choice, in one sentence
Options consideredEach rejected option with a concrete reason for rejection. Not "we preferred the chosen approach" but the token count that exceeded the window, the cost per call, the reason a similarity search was the wrong shape for the retrieval this task needs
ConsequencesPositive and negative, whether the decision is reversible, and what would make us revisit it. The revisit trigger is what keeps the record alive rather than historical
Assumptions to validateEach one paired with the measurement that would validate it, which links the record to the eval suite and the rollout plan
The skeleton, for the one you write in the lab 
# ADR-001: Retrieval strategy for the review assistant

## Status
Proposed

## Context
[Baselines and constraints from discovery, with numbers. What we do not yet know.]

## Decision
[The choice, in one sentence.]

## Options considered
1. [Option] Rejected: [concrete reason, with the number that killed it]
2. [Option] Rejected: [concrete reason]
3. [Option] Chosen.

## Consequences
Positive: [...]  Negative: [...]  Reversible? [...]
What would make us revisit this? [...]

## Assumptions to validate in phase 1
[Each with the measurement that validates it.]
Next

Lab 8, step 2, is one ADR for the single most consequential choice in a real engagement of your own, with at least two rejected options carrying concrete reasons and a stated revisit trigger. Twenty minutes. It is the cheapest way to stop confusing a decision record with a design document, which is the distinction section 7's engineering audience turns on.

Judge your own handover by asking what the receiving team would have to reverse-engineer. Anything on that list is a missing artefact, and the one most often on it is the changelog.
