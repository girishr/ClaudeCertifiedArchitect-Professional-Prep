Recap and what is next

The takeaways, the heuristics, the distractors, a closed-book self-test, and where Module 5 picks up.

Exam domain 6From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 4 / Section 08 of 8

Six takeaways, and one habit running under all of them: write the thing down before it is contested. Almost every failure in this domain is an unwritten assumption meeting a disappointed person.

The six takeaways
#TakeawayThe operative detail
01Design against the underlying job, not the stated askThe ask is a clue about what someone has seen elsewhere. The job and the success measure are the specification. When they diverge, say so out loud rather than quietly substituting one for the other
02Acceptance criteria are agreed before the pilot, in writingMetric, dataset, threshold, measurement method, signatory. When a stakeholder moves the goalposts, the answer is to pause and agree the criteria, never to argue the number
03You commit to targets and processes, never to guaranteesAn accuracy target on a named eval set, with a confidence interval and a fallback. In the contract: availability, p50/p95/p99, and a quality process. Never a contractual accuracy percentage on open-ended generation
04The stage you are in decides what the right action isPOC proves feasibility, pilot proves value, production adds SLOs, on-call and rollback. The same action is diligent at one stage and over-engineering at another, and the ramp retires one unknown per phase
05A handoff is judged by whether someone else can change the system safelyVersioned prompts with a changelog, and an eval suite in CI. Those two are one deliverable. Everything else on the artefact list protects them
06Bad news travels early and arrives with optionsDiagnose first, because a large share of misses are measurement problems. Then report with a recommendation. Descoping to the high-confidence subset is usually better than missing the target on everything

8.2The distractors, by the sentence they hide behind

Read this list for its common shape. Every entry is the most accommodating available response to the person in the room, which is what makes it hard to eliminate under time pressure.

Eleven wrong answers that read as good client service
The optionWhy it fails
"Extend the pilot by a quarter"With no change to hypothesis, scope or measurement. Time is not an intervention
"Build the full production architecture during discovery"Over-engineering before value is proven. Match investment to the stage
"Present the architecture to the executive sponsor"Wrong artefact for the audience. He wants the recommendation and three numbers
"Commit to 99 percent accuracy in the SLA"Open-ended generation quality is not contractually guaranteeable, however good the pilot number was
"Train users more"When the workflow itself got worse for them, training accelerates a rejection
"Escalate to the steering committee"Before diagnosing. Escalation without a recommendation reads as abdication
"Start with the highest-value use case"Ignoring feasibility. High value with low feasibility is the quadrant where pilots die
"Swap to a bigger model"Before checking whether the eval set is representative of real inputs
"Hand over prompts in a shared doc"Prompts need versioning, evals and a changelog. Unversioned prompts are the top handoff failure
"Roll out to everyone because the pilot went well"Pilot distribution rarely survives the full population
"Let the sponsor lower the target"Without recording that it moved and why. A silent change is worse than no target

8.3Two structural tells specific to this domain

  • Select TWO items here usually pair one technical action with one stakeholder action. Fix the eval set and tell the sponsor. Route the long tail to humans and record the scope change. If both your picks are technical, or both are conversations, re-read.
  • The stage named in the stem is the strongest filter available. Two options can both describe good practice, with only one of them appropriate during discovery or during a pilot or after go-live. Find the stage word before you read the options, and treat "before proceeding" as a likely sequencing key.

8.4Closed-book self-test

No scrolling. Anything you cannot produce cleanly is the section to reread, not the whole module.

  1. Separate the stated ask, the underlying job and the success measure for "we want a chatbot on our docs", and say what you do when the first two diverge.
  2. Name the five elements of an agreed acceptance criterion, and say what "good enough" is defined against.
  3. List the six components of cost per task, and name the two that teams forget.
  4. State what an SLA may commit to, and the one commitment it must never contain. Then say why that one is not defensible.
  5. Write the ROI formula, then apply the adoption discount to a 40 percent saving at 30 percent adoption.
  6. Name the eight pilot-to-production gates.
  7. Say what POC, pilot and production each prove, then name the four rollout phases in order and the unknown each one retires.
  8. Walk the five moves when a pilot misses its target, in order, and name the three things you never do.

Two more worth adding if the first eight came easily: reproduce the audience matrix from section 7 from memory, including the "avoid" column, and give the exec message and the compliance message in one line each.

Then go and write the artefacts. Lab 8 is 120 minutes for a discovery interview, an ADR, a metrics table, a phased rollout plan, an exec one-pager and a team enablement set. This domain rewards format discipline, and format discipline comes from having produced the format once under a clock.
