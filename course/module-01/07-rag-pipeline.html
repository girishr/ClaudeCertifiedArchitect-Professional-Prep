RAG pipeline design

Chunking by what the corpus is, indexing by how queries are phrased.

Official screens S19 to S20~18 min in the original
CCAR-P course / Module 1 / Section 07 of 12

Section 6 named RAG as a known-good shape and showed where it is misapplied. This goes one level down: how a corpus is broken up, how chunks are made findable, and how the strategy is matched to the questions the system will actually be asked.

7.1Chunking is chosen by what the corpus is

A chunk is the unit that gets retrieved. The approach follows the structure of the source material, not a default size.

Three chunking approaches
ApproachHow it worksWhen it earns its place
Fixed-sizeUniform spans with overlap, regardless of structureHomogeneous unstructured text with weak natural boundaries. Simplest to operate
SemanticSplits on meaning boundaries, topic shifts, sentence groups that hang togetherProse where a retrieved chunk has to be self-contained to answer well. Reduces mid-idea cuts
HierarchicalPreserves document structure, sections and subsections, and retrieves at the level that fitsStructured documents, contracts, manuals, policies, where section context carries meaning

7.2Indexing is chosen by how queries are phrased

Three indexing strategies
StrategyWhat it matchesWhen it earns its place
Dense, embeddingsSemantic similarity. Meaning, not wordsQueries phrased differently from the source: paraphrase, intent, concept matching
Sparse, keyword such as BM25Exact terms, identifiers, codes, namesQueries that hinge on a specific token: part numbers, statute citations, error codes
HybridBoth, combinedMixed query patterns, which is the common production case. Recovers the exact matches dense retrieval misses

A hybrid index returns two ranked lists that have to be merged into one. Reciprocal rank fusion is the standard low-tuning way to do it: each result is scored by its rank in each list, and the combined score favours items that rank well in both. For an architect the point is that merging dense and sparse is a design decision with a known, defensible default, not an open research problem you have to solve on the call.

7.3The trade-off you have to be able to state

Every retrieval design trades three things against each other: retrieval quality (does the right chunk come back), latency (how much retrieval adds per request), and maintenance (what it costs to keep correct as the corpus grows).

Exercise, from the module

A professional-services partner has about 4,000 documents: client engagement contracts (structured, section-numbered), past project write-ups (long-form prose), and a methodology handbook (structured, with defined procedures). Their team asks three kinds of question: what does our methodology say about X?, find the termination clause in the Acme contract, and summarise how we have handled engagements like this one. Name the chunking approach per document type, the indexing strategy for the corpus, and the trade-off you are making. Write it down before you read on.

The model answer
DecisionAnswerBecause
Contracts and handbookHierarchicalBoth are structured and section-numbered, so retrieving at section level keeps a clause or procedure intact and self-contained. Fixed-size cuts across section boundaries and loses the context the query depends on
Project write-upsSemanticLong-form prose has no section numbers to anchor hierarchical chunking, and fixed-size cuts mid-idea. Semantic keeps each passage coherent enough to answer alone
IndexingHybrid, dense plus sparseSparse handles the exact-target lookups where an identifier matters. Dense handles concept lookup and synthesis. Neither alone covers both
The dominant trade-offQuery variety is the load-bearing constraintHybrid adds retrieval compute and a rank-fusion step. Those costs earn their place only because the query set genuinely needs both modes

A support team indexes its 900-page product manual for an assistant. Users report that questions phrased in their own words work well, but any question naming a specific error code returns unrelated passages. Latency is comfortably inside budget and the corpus changes about twice a year.

What is the right change?

  1. Move to a larger embedding model
  2. Shorten the index refresh interval
  3. Reduce the chunk size and increase overlap
  4. Add a sparse index alongside the dense one and fuse the results
Go hybrid. The symptom names the diagnosis: paraphrased questions work, exact identifiers fail, which is dense retrieval doing exactly what dense retrieval does. The latency headroom is the detail that makes it affordable, and the slow-changing corpus is the detail that makes the added maintenance tolerable. Read those two facts as permission, not as scenery.
