Items in this part of the domain have one shape. A regulation is named, a scenario is given, and you choose the control set that satisfies it. The distractors are real controls that satisfy a different obligation, which is why the mapping has to be held precisely rather than approximately.

5.1Four rows people get wrong

Art. 22 is about intervention, not explanation. The enforceable duty is meaningful information about the logic, under Arts. 13 to 15. The strong "right to explanation" phrasing sits in Recital 71, not in the Art. 22 text, so do not overstate it. What Art. 22 gets you architecturally is a human with authority to overturn the decision before it takes effect.

SOC 2 Type II is the one that proves controls operated. Type I proves they were designed. If a stem asks for evidence of operating effectiveness over a period, Type I is the wrong artefact.

A DPA is not a transfer mechanism. A DPA covers processor duties. A cross-border transfer to a non-adequate destination needs a Chapter V mechanism, or you keep the processing in-region instead.

An authorised path is not an authorisation. Bedrock in GovCloud makes a FedRAMP High path available to you. Your own system still needs its own authorisation, and a vendor attestation covers the vendor, not your application.

Watch out

"Log everything, forever" fails two regimes at once. Over-retention creates its own GDPR and DPDP exposure and expands the blast radius of any breach. Retention limits are a control, not a gap in one.

Watch out

If a practice question says high-risk EU AI Act obligations bite in August 2026, it predates the Digital Omnibus deferral. August 2026 is the transparency and general-applicability date. Annex III high-risk moved to December 2027.

Directional, not gospel. The EU AI Act sub-deadlines are the part of this domain most likely to have moved again. The phasing order is stable; re-check the exact dates against a primary source rather than trusting any study kit, including this one.

A German motor insurer wants Claude to pre-assess inbound claims. The system's output determines whether a claim is declined, and today's design sends the decline letter to the policyholder automatically with no human touch. Volume is around 4,000 claims a week. Legal has signed a DPA with the model provider and asks the architect whether anything further is needed on the decision-making side.

Which control set actually satisfies the obligation in play?

  1. Publish a system card and attach the model's chain of thought to each decline, to satisfy the right to explanation
  2. Route every decline to a qualified claims handler who can overturn it before it takes effect, disclose the AI involvement to the policyholder, and log the inputs, prompt and model version and the human who signed off
  3. Rely on the signed DPA and enable zero data retention on the claims traffic
  4. Apply a 5 percent risk-weighted post-hoc review with a bias dashboard across protected attributes
Human intervention with authority to overturn, plus disclosure. The volume figure is there to make sampling attractive and the DPA is there to make the question look answered. Neither touches the duty: this is a solely automated decision with a significant effect on a person, and the control is a qualified human in front of it. The bias dashboard in option D is a good idea that satisfies a different obligation.

Work the mapping in one direction only. Name the obligation, then name the thing you build. An answer that names a document where the obligation demands a mechanism is the distractor, every time.
