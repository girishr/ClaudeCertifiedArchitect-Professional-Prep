Human in the loop

Where the human goes, decided by reversibility, with blast radius as the tiebreaker.

Exam domain 5From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 3 / Section 03 of 8

Nobody fails this domain by forgetting the human. They fail it by putting the human in the wrong place: a gate in front of work that could simply have been undone, or sampled review behind an action that moved money out of the company.

Placement is the whole question, and one property decides it. Can the action be undone cheaply? If not, the human goes before it. If it can, spend the same effort on making the undo reliable and watch the output afterwards.

Five situations and the pattern each one calls for
SituationPatternThe operative detail
Irreversible, externally visible. Payment, email to a customer, regulatory filing, deletion, contract Synchronous approval gate before the action Gate the action, not the reasoning. Approving a plan is cheaper than approving each step
Reversible with cheap rollback. A draft, an internal ticket, a staged change Post-hoc sampled review plus easy undo Build the undo before you build the gate
High volume, low unit severity Statistical sampling, risk-weighted Sample more where confidence is low or value is high. Reviewing 100% destroys the ROI and is a common wrong answer
Regulated decision affecting a person. Credit, hiring, medical, legal, insurance A qualified human reviews before the decision takes effect, plus disclosure that AI assisted This is both a regulatory requirement and an Anthropic Usage Policy requirement. Two independent reasons, one design
Novel or ambiguous cases the model flags Escalation path with an SLA, a defined owner, and a feedback loop back into evals Escalation without a named owner and a time bound is theatre

3.1Approve the plan, not every step

For multi-step agents, plan-level approval beats step-level approval. Approving a full plan before execution gives the human meaningful influence at the point where influence is possible, which is before anything has happened.

Step-by-step confirmation looks stricter and is worse. It trains users to click through, and a reviewer who clicks through is worse than no reviewer at all, because the design now carries false assurance: the log says a human approved each action, and nobody did.

Watch out

If a stem tells you reviewers approve 98 percent of items unchanged, that is not a detail of scale. It is the answer. The reviewer is adding no signal, so move to risk-weighted sampling and reinvest the saved effort in evals. Full review is only correct when volume is low, severity is high, and the reviewer genuinely adds signal.

A logistics firm runs a Claude workflow over inbound carrier invoices. Two outcomes are possible per invoice: attach a discrepancy note to an internal dispute ticket, or release payment to the carrier through the payments API. Every output currently goes to a two-person review queue, which approves 97 percent unchanged and is eight working days behind. Finance wants the queue gone and has asked for a design that clears the backlog without "taking the humans out".

What review design should the architect propose?

  1. Keep reviewing 100 percent of outputs and add two more reviewers to clear the backlog
  2. A synchronous approval gate on payment release only, with risk-weighted sampling plus easy undo on the dispute-note path
  3. Remove human review, tighten the system prompt to forbid incorrect payments, and add a payment-anomaly dashboard
  4. Require step-by-step human confirmation for every tool call the workflow makes
Split the paths by reversibility. The stem hides one irreversible external action inside a high-volume, low-severity stream, and the 97 percent unchanged rate tells you the current uniform gate is paying for nothing on most items. One design, two branches: gate the payment, sample the note, and make sure the note can be pulled back.

Ask where the undo lives before you ask where the reviewer sits. If there is a cheap undo, the reviewer can stand behind the action. If there is not, someone has to stand in front of it, and the design has to make that person's approval mean something.
