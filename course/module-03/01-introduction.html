Designing the safety stack

Safety in this domain is something you build, not something you write down.

Exam domain 5From this kit's domain notes
CCAR-P course / Module 3 / Section 01 of 8

An enterprise buyer asks what happens when the system gets it wrong. The weak answer is a document: a policy, an acceptable-use statement, a commitment to review. The answer this domain trains is a set of components with names, positions in the request path, and costs you can defend in a budget conversation.

That is the shift. Governance here is not a layer laid over the architecture after it works. It is part of the architecture, and every control you add has a latency number, a money number, and something it demonstrably cannot catch.

1.1What the exam is actually testing

Not whether you can list controls. Whether you can pick a proportionate stack and justify it. Almost every distractor in this domain is a legitimate control that is either too heavy for the stated risk, which kills the business case, or too light, which leaves unacceptable residual risk.

The examiner wants an architect who can say "reversible, low blast radius, internal, so sampled review plus logging is enough" and equally "irreversible external financial action, so a synchronous gate is non-negotiable even at a latency cost". Both sentences are correct answers. Which one is correct depends entirely on what the stem told you.

Read every stem for three signals

Who is harmed, can it be undone, and what lifecycle stage are we in. Those three usually determine the key before you have read a single option. The second one carries the most weight, and it is the spine of this whole module: when a governance question is genuinely close, the answer that preserves the ability to undo is the one to pick.

Certain words in a stem are load-bearing and should change your reading of every option underneath them: irreversible, externally visible, regulated, customer-facing, PHI, minors, autonomous. Their presence raises the floor on what an acceptable answer looks like. Their absence, particularly alongside a mention of latency or cost pressure, lowers the ceiling.
